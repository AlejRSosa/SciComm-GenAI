Task 1 · Warm-up: your core message

Before changing formats, pin down what must not change: the key message, the facts, and the words your audience needs explained. Copilot's answer to this prompt is your core message. You will reuse it in Tasks 3–6.

Example prompt (P-T-C-F-A + Accessibility)
PERSONA: You are a science communicator who plans content for several formats (images, infographics, interactive content, social media).
TASK: Turn my text below into a core message that I will reuse in every format.
CONTEXT: My topic is [how bacteria share antibiotic-resistance genes on plasmids]. My audience is [adults with no science background, reading age about 12].
FORMAT: A two-column table containing:
1. Key message as an inverted-pyramid lead: one sentence, under 20 words, most important point first.
2. Three supporting facts.
3. Five technical terms, each with a plain-English definition under 15 words.
4. One everyday analogy.
5. One common misconception and its correction.
ACCURACY: Use ONLY facts in my text and quote the sentence each fact comes from. Do not add new facts. Flag anything uncertain.
ACCESSIBILITY: Plain English; expand acronyms on first use.

My text:
[paste a short text on your topic, e.g. from Session 1]
Why “use only facts in my text”? Asking Copilot to quote its source sentence makes invented facts easy to spot. If a “fact” has no matching sentence, delete it.

Task 2 · Image → text: let Copilot read a figure

Multimodal works in both directions. Before generating images, use Copilot to interpret one: explain a scientific figure for your audience and write its alt text.

Why alt text matters

Alt text (alternative text) is a short written description attached to an image. Most readers never see it. It is read aloud by screen readers, shown when an image fails to load, and used by search engines to understand what the image shows.

Without it, a figure is a blank space for anyone who can't see it. Over two million people in the UK live with sight loss (RNIB). Screen readers are also used by people with other disabilities, such as cognitive, learning, hearing or motor disabilities, and about 1 in 10 screen-reader users report no disability at all (WebAIM Screen Reader User Survey #10, 2024). In science communication the figure often is the finding. A missing or vague alt text can therefore mean the reader misses the main result.

It is also a legal and professional expectation, not an optional extra:

  • The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) make text alternatives for non-text content their very first success criterion (WCAG 2.2, Success Criterion 1.1.1 Non-text Content, Level A).
  • UK public sector bodies, including universities, must meet these standards on their websites and documents (The Public Sector Bodies (Websites and Mobile Applications) (No. 2) Accessibility Regulations 2018).
  • Most journals, funders and social media platforms now ask for alt text or image descriptions.

Good alt text answers one question: what does a reader need to get from this image, in this place?

  • The same figure needs different alt text in a lecture than in a press release.
  • A chart's alt text gives the takeaway, not the colours of the bars.
  • A purely decorative image gets empty alt text, so screen readers skip it.
  • A complex figure needs a short alt text and a longer description nearby.

Do it

  1. Find a figure on your topic that you made yourself or that is openly licensed (e.g. an open-access paper under CC BY). Take a screenshot or download it.
  2. Upload it to Copilot (paperclip / “+” icon) with the prompt below.
  3. If the figure has been obtained from a paper, compare Copilot's reading with the figure legend in the original paper.
Example prompt (P-T-C-F-A + Accessibility)
PERSONA: You are a science communicator who writes accessible descriptions of scientific figures.
TASK: Interpret the attached figure and write alt text for it.
CONTEXT: The figure is from [paper title, authors, year, licence]. It will appear in material for [adults with no science background].
FORMAT:
1. What the figure shows: chart type, axes, units, groups, main trend.
2. What it means for my audience, in 3 sentences.
3. Short alt text (about 125 characters) and a long description (3–5 sentences with the key values).
ACCURACY: Describe only what you can see. If you can't read a label or value, say so; don't guess. Don't claim causation the figure doesn't show.
ACCESSIBILITY: The alt text gives the figure's PURPOSE (its takeaway), not just its appearance. Don't start with "Image of".
Accessibility built in A chart's alt text should give the takeaway (“Resistance rose from 5% to 30% between 2010 and 2020”), not “a line graph in blue”. The ~125-character figure is a common rule of thumb, not a hard limit. Data-heavy figures also need a long description.

Think it through: is this alt text any good?

Don't accept Copilot's alt text because it sounds fluent. Answer these questions in your lab log.

  1. Purpose: Why is this figure here, and what should the reader take away? Does the alt text deliver that takeaway, or just describe what the figure looks like?
  2. Equivalence: Close your eyes and have someone read you the alt text. Would you come away with the same understanding as a sighted reader looking at the figure?
  3. Accuracy: Is every number, label and trend in the alt text actually in the figure? Has Copilot added anything it can't see, or turned a correlation into a cause?
  4. Completeness: Is anything important missing, such as the direction of change, the groups being compared, units, or uncertainty (error bars, confidence intervals)?
  5. Context: Does it repeat what the caption or surrounding text already says? Would it change if the figure appeared in a press release instead of a lecture?
  6. Language: Is it concise, in plain English, without “image of” or “picture of”? Is colour mentioned only where colour carries meaning?
  7. Text in the image: If the figure contains words that matter (axis titles, labels), are they included?

Not every image can go into an AI

Uploading a figure to Copilot makes a copy of it and sends it to an AI service for processing. Whether you are allowed to do that depends on who owns the image and its licence, not on whether you can find it online.

  1. Which of these could you upload, and why?
    • (a) a figure you made for your own BSc dissertation
    • (b) a figure from an open-access paper under CC BY
    • (c) a figure from a paywalled journal article
    • (d) a photo from a Google Images search
    • (e) a diagram scanned from a textbook
  2. For (b), what does the licence require you to do when you reuse the figure? How would the answer change for a CC BY-NC or CC BY-ND licence?
  3. If your figure has already been published, do you still own it, or did you transfer copyright to the publisher?

Where do AI models get their knowledge? Large language and image models are trained on enormous collections of text and images, much of it gathered from the public web, alongside licensed and synthetic data. Developers rarely disclose exactly what was included, and much of that material was created by people who were never asked. With your UofG account, Copilot's enterprise data protection means your prompts and uploads are not used to train Microsoft's foundation models. That protects your data, but it doesn't answer whether you have the right to upload someone else's work.

Keep your answers: we will come back to who owns training data, consent and copyright in the upcoming ethics lecture.

Task 3 · Text → image: one explanatory illustration

Generate one image that makes the key message from your Task 1 core message easier to grasp. Then write its alt text, and check whether that alt text describes what Copilot actually drew.

Before you generate: image generation has a cost

Environmental cost

Generating an image takes far more computing power than generating text. In one study of open-source models, image generation used on average over 60 times more energy than text generation. The least efficient model used roughly half a smartphone charge per image (Luccioni et al., 2024). Copilot's own figures are not published, but every “just one more try” adds up. Plan your prompt before you press enter.

Artists and copyright

Image generators learn from millions of existing images, many made by artists, illustrators and photographers who never consented and are not paid. Whether this is lawful is still being fought out in the courts. In the UK, Getty Images v Stability AI (High Court, November 2025) left many of the central questions about training unresolved. Generated images can also closely imitate a living artist's style.

We test image generation today so you understand what it can and can't do. Both debates are covered fully in the upcoming ethics lecture.

Example prompt (P-T-C-F-A + Accessibility)
PERSONA: You are a scientific illustrator who makes images for public health leaflets.
TASK: Create one image showing [one rod-shaped bacterium passing a small ring of DNA (a plasmid) to a neighbouring bacterium through a thin tube-like bridge].
CONTEXT: For a public leaflet about [antibiotic resistance], read by [adults with no science background].
FORMAT: Flat 2D vector illustration, clean simple shapes, plain white background, landscape 16:9, subject centred with empty space around it for labels I will add later.
ACCURACY: Biologically plausible shapes and proportions; exactly one bridge and one plasmid.
ACCESSIBILITY: Colour-blind-safe palette ([blue and orange] only, never red against green); strong contrast with the background; NO text, letters, labels or numbers anywhere in the image.
Why “no text in the image”? Image generators often misspell words or invent fake lettering. Text baked into a picture also can't be read by a screen reader, resized, or translated. Add labels yourself as real text, in PowerPoint or Word, next to or over the image.

Iterate

This is chain prompting with images. Change one element per follow-up so you can tell what made the difference, e.g. “Same image, but make the plasmid larger and the bridge more visible.” Record each change in your log. Keep the number of attempts low; see the cost explanation above.

Then: alt text from the image itself

Chain prompt 2 (upload the generated image in a fresh chat)
TASK: Look ONLY at the attached image, not at my earlier prompt. Describe what is actually visible.
FORMAT: Then write short alt text (about 125 characters).
CONTEXT: It will sit next to a paragraph explaining [how bacteria share resistance genes].
ACCURACY: If anything in the image is biologically wrong or unclear, list it.

Watch for this

 If you ask for alt text in the same chat where you described the image, Copilot may describe what you asked for rather than what it drew. Uploading the image in a fresh chat is a fairer test, and the gap between the two descriptions is worth noting in your write-up.

To examine the differences between a fresh chat and the one that you used to generate the image, prompt the AI to generate the alt text in both, and discuss the differences.

Task 4 · An accessible infographic

Use Copilot's Create area (Poster / Infographic) to lay out the key message, two facts and the correction from your Task 1 core message. Then fix what image generation does badly: text. The cost explanation in Task 3 applies here too.

  1. In Copilot, open Create from the left-hand menu and choose Infographic or Poster. Or ask in chat. Menu names may differ slightly.
  2. Run the prompt below with exact wording for every text element.
  3. Use Edit to make one targeted change at a time.
  4. Rebuild the version you'll actually use: generate the visual elements only, then add the text as real text in PowerPoint.
Example prompt (P-T-C-F-A + Accessibility)
PERSONA: You are an information designer for public health campaigns.
TASK: Create a portrait infographic about [how bacteria share antibiotic-resistance genes].
CONTEXT: For [adults with no science background], seen on [a GP surgery noticeboard].
FORMAT: Three panels top to bottom, numbered 1–3, one simple icon per panel. Use exactly this text and no other text:
Title: "[key message, shortened, e.g. Bacteria can share resistance]"
Panel 1: "[Fact 1, shortened, e.g. Some bacteria carry genes that resist antibiotics]"
Panel 2: "[Fact 3, shortened, e.g. They can pass these genes to neighbours]"
Panel 3: "[the correction, e.g. It's the bacteria that become resistant, not you]"
ACCURACY: Reproduce the text exactly. No extra words, numbers or claims.
ACCESSIBILITY: Large sans-serif text, dark text on a light background, colour-blind-safe [blue/orange] palette, no decorative clutter, clear top-to-bottom reading order.
Accessibility built in: the two-layer method
  1. Ask Copilot for the icons or illustration only, with no text.
  2. Open PowerPoint and create a new blank presentation.
  3. Put them into a PowerPoint slide (set to portrait) and type the title and panel text as real text boxes.
  4. Add alt text to each icon, or mark it decorative.
  5. Run Review → Check Accessibility and fix what it flags (contrast, reading order, missing alt text).
This avoids spelling errors in the image and makes the text readable by screen readers, resizable and editable.

Task 5 · Interactive: a working “check your understanding” quiz

Copilot can write code, so you can create interactive learning without being a programmer. You'll get a single web page with a quiz, instant feedback, and a score. Then you'll test whether it's genuinely accessible.

How to use this prompt The PERSONA to ACCESSIBILITY lines are the prompt. At the end, replace [paste your core message from Task 1] with the table Copilot gave you in Task 1 (from your lab log). That way Copilot works from your checked facts, not its own.
Example prompt (P-T-C-F-A + Accessibility)
PERSONA: You are a web developer who builds accessible e-learning activities.
TASK: Write a single, self-contained HTML file (HTML, CSS and JavaScript in one file; no external libraries) for a 5-question multiple-choice quiz.
CONTEXT: Topic: [how bacteria share antibiotic-resistance genes]. Learners: [adults with no science background]. They will open the file by double-clicking it in their browser.
FORMAT: One question at a time with a "Next" button; instant feedback after each answer explaining WHY it is right or wrong (1–2 sentences); a score at the end and a "Try again" button.
ACCURACY: Base every question only on my core message below. Include its misconception as one of the wrong options. Every correct answer must be supported by my core message.
ACCESSIBILITY (WCAG 2.2 AA):
- Fully usable with the keyboard only; real <button> elements; clearly visible focus outline.
- Feedback in words and symbols (✓ Correct / ✗ Not quite), not colour alone.
- Feedback announced to screen readers with an aria-live region.
- Text contrast at least 4.5:1; base font size at least 18px; works at 200% zoom and on a phone.
- Respect prefers-reduced-motion. Set lang="en-GB" and a meaningful page title.

My core message:
[paste your core message from Task 1]

Save and run it

  1. Copy the whole code block. If Copilot stops part-way, type “continue from where you stopped”.
  2. Paste into Notepad → File → Save as → name it quiz.html, set “Save as type” to All files and encoding to UTF-8.
  3. Double-click to open it in your browser.

Test it yourself

Critical thinking point

 Ask Copilot: “Review your code against WCAG 2.2 AA and list any failures.” Then compare its answer with your own tests. Copilot will often say the code is compliant when your keyboard test shows it isn't. Record any mismatch in your log.

Task 6 · One message, many platforms

Each platform has its own length, format, audience and accessibility tools. Repurpose your core message for three platforms without the facts drifting as they're shortened.

Step A: Platform brief first

Example prompt (P-T-C-F-A + Accessibility)
PERSONA: You are a social media manager for a science communication organisation.
TASK: Before writing any posts, compare [Instagram (carousel), LinkedIn and Bluesky] for sharing my core message.
CONTEXT: My audiences are [the general public on Instagram and Bluesky; healthcare professionals on LinkedIn].
FORMAT: A table with: typical audience, best format for this message, text length limits, how to add alt text, how hashtags are used, one misinformation risk on that platform.
ACCURACY: Mark anything you are unsure of, or that may have changed recently, as "verify".

Check the length limits and alt-text steps yourself on each platform's help pages. Limits change, and Copilot's information may be out of date.

Step B: The content pack

How to use this prompt The PERSONA to ACCESSIBILITY lines are the prompt. At the end, replace [paste your core message from Task 1] with the table Copilot gave you in Task 1 (from your lab log). That way Copilot works from your checked facts, not its own.
Example prompt (P-T-C-F-A + Accessibility)
PERSONA: You are a social media manager for a science communication organisation.
TASK: Using ONLY my core message below, write:
1. Instagram carousel: 5 slides (max 15 words each), a caption, and alt text for each slide.
2. LinkedIn post (150–200 words) with a clear call to action.
3. Bluesky thread: 3 posts, each within the platform's character limit.
CONTEXT: [General public on Instagram and Bluesky; healthcare professionals on LinkedIn.]
FORMAT: Inverted pyramid: every post opens with the key message.
ACCURACY: Keep every caveat from my core message. Add no new facts or numbers.
ACCESSIBILITY:
- Hashtags in CamelCase (e.g. #AntibioticResistance), max 3, at the end.
- Max 2 emojis per post, never mid-sentence, never replacing words.
- No "fancy font" Unicode characters.
- Plain English; define any technical term on first use.

My core message:
[paste your core message from Task 1]

Step C: Drift check (a fact-checking chain, Session 1 Pattern 3)

Fact-checking chain
Prompt 1: Compare each post with my core message. List any statement that is new, exaggerated, missing a caveat, or changes the meaning. Quote the post text and the core message text side by side.

Prompt 2: How would [a microbiologist] critique these posts? What uncertainties or limitations should be acknowledged?

Then do your own check. Shortening is where hedges disappear: “can contribute to” becomes “causes”.

Why these accessibility rules?
  • Screen readers read CamelCase hashtags as separate words; #antibioticresistance comes out as one long garbled word.
  • Each emoji is read aloud by name (“face with tears of joy”), so emojis mid-sentence break the flow.
  • “Fancy font” text is made of maths symbols that screen readers read letter by letter, or skip.

Be sceptical

 If you ask Copilot for “the best time to post” or “how the algorithm works”, you'll get confident generalities that are hard to verify. Treat them as hypotheses, not facts.

Evaluate your outputs

You can use these five questions to ensure that you are generating an adequate output.

Evaluation criteria
CriterionAsk yourself
AccuracyIs every claim traceable to a source? Did the visual introduce a scientific error (wrong shape, scale or process)?
Audience fitRight reading level, tone and format for this audience on this platform?
AccessibilityAlt text, contrast, colour use, keyboard use: built in or missing?
RepresentationDo generated people or places reinforce stereotypes (e.g. every scientist looks the same)?

Summary

Disclose

Label AI-generated images and media, e.g. “Illustration generated with Microsoft Copilot and edited by [name].” Some platforms also have their own AI-content labels.

Protect

Use your UofG login only. No personal data, no unpublished results, no photos of real people.

Credit

Uploaded figures need a source and licence. Don't ask Copilot to copy a recognisable artist's style or a brand's characters.

Verify

You are the author, so you are responsible for every fact. “Copilot said so” is not a source.
