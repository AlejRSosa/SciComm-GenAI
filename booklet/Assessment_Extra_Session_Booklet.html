Before you start

In Session 3 you analysed a dataset in Excel and charted it yourself, with Copilot as a chat partner. Today covers the parts of your Crisis Information Pack that come before that: finding sources and datasets, and cleaning the data with a change log. We work through one short case study from start to finish: one dataset and one paper.

How today works Think first, then use GenAI to check or challenge your work, not to replace it. Use it when it actually adds to your research skills, and write down what you did. Doing a step yourself is always allowed, as long as you explain it in your report.

Ground rules
  • UofG Copilot is the only AI service the University approves for University data (UofG IT). For this assessment you may use another research platform, but you must name it and explain how you used it.
  • Every number comes from your spreadsheet; every reference from a database or publisher. “Copilot said so” is not a source.
  • Today's data are public and contain no personal information. Never upload personal, unpublished or confidential material (Session 3, Task 2).
  • Never edit the Raw Data sheet. Every change happens on Clean Data and goes in the Cleaning Log.

Running example used in this booklet

You are an adviser in the Prime Minister's Office, preparing a pack on measles in England. In January 2026 the WHO announced that the UK had lost its measles elimination status, based on the spread of cases in 2024 (iHV). England had 2,911 laboratory-confirmed cases in 2024, the highest since 2012, and 959 in 2025. From 1 January to 9 November 2026 there were 1,055 (UKHSA). Two children died of measles in England in the first half of 2026 (UKHSA). In 2025–26, 83.8% of five-year-olds had had both MMR doses, and no vaccine in the childhood schedule now meets the WHO 95% target (UKHSA).

To keep the case study short, you work with just two sources:

  • Paper: Jary H, Pullen A, et al. Sociodemographic inequalities in the epidemiology and vaccine uptake within a large outbreak of measles in Birmingham, England, 2023 to 2024. Euro Surveill. 2025;30(16):2400652 (open access, CC BY 4.0). You will find it in PubMed yourself in Task 1.

Task 1 · How to find a suitable dataset

In your assessment, you will need to find one dataset and source papers, and record how you found them. You choose whether to search on your own or with AI help; either is fine if you explain it.

  1. Find a dataset using the table below. For the case study, open the UKHSA historic measles page you used in Set up.
  2. Search the literature on your own, with AI help, or both (see the two routes below).
  3. Record every search in your search log and list the sources you keep.

Why write your question first?

Your question decides which data and papers are relevant. If Copilot writes it, you lose the thinking the Research section is marked on.

Search the literature: two routes

Both routes are allowed. The marks come from explaining what you did and why, not from which route you chose.

RouteWhat you doWhat you write in your report

Example: using the MeSH database

MeSH (Medical Subject Headings) is the controlled vocabulary used to index PubMed. One MeSH term collects papers that use different wording for the same idea.

  1. Go to the MeSH database and type immunization coverage.
  2. It leads to the MeSH term Vaccination Coverage, defined as the rate of vaccination by geography or demography. Its entry terms include “Immunization Coverage” (MeSH record).
  3. Add it to the PubMed search builder, or type "Vaccination Coverage"[mh] in PubMed. Papers indexed with this term are found whichever wording the authors used.

Example: Boolean operators in PubMed

Boolean operators and symbols in PubMed with examples
Operator or symbolWhat it doesExample

Put together, a search for the case study paper could be: (measles[mh] OR measles[tiab]) AND outbreak[tiab] AND (Birmingham[tiab] OR England[tiab]), filtered to 2023–2025. See the PubMed User Guide for details.

PERSONA: You are a health sciences librarian who teaches literature searching.
TASK: Help me build a PubMed search for my research question. Do not search for, list or summarise any papers.
CONTEXT: My research question is: [your research question]. My own first search was: [your own search string].
FORMAT:
1. A table: concept | MeSH term | free-text synonyms.
2. One search string using AND, OR and brackets.
3. Two PubMed filters worth considering, and why.
ACCURACY: Only suggest MeSH terms you are confident exist, and remind me to check each one in the MeSH database. Do not give any references, paper titles or author names.
ACCESSIBILITY: Plain English; explain every symbol you use ([mh], [tiab], *, quotation marks) in words.

Think it through

  1. Did every suggested MeSH term exist when you checked it in the MeSH database?
  2. Compare your own search with the suggested one: what did Copilot add, and what did it miss?
  3. Did this route actually add to your searching skills, or would you have got there faster on your own?

Critical thinking point: asking a chatbot for references

Chatbots can produce references that look real but don't exist. In one study, 18% of the citations GPT-4 produced for short literature reviews were fabricated, and 24% of the real ones had substantive errors (Walters & Wilder, 2023). That is why the prompt above asks for a search string, not a reading list.

  1. If Copilot names a paper, can you find it in PubMed with the same title, authors, journal and year?
  2. Does the paper say what Copilot said it says? Read the abstract yourself.
  3. How would you report a reference you could not verify? (You leave it out.)

Task 2 · Read with Copilot: assistant, not reader

Read the paper's abstract yourself, then use Copilot to pull out its findings, limitations and gaps. Check them against the paper, and check the paper against your dataset.

  1. Read the abstract of Jary et al. (2025) yourself. Write two sentences in your lab log, in your own words. These are your own notes.
  2. Download the PDF. It is open access under CC BY 4.0 (Eurosurveillance), so you may upload it.
  3. Open a fresh chat, upload the PDF (paperclip / “+” icon) and run the prompt below.
  4. Verify three claims against the paper, and record what you found.
  5. Cross-check with your dataset. Birmingham is in the West Midlands. Does the outbreak show up in the West Midlands figures for 2023 and 2024?
Why read the abstract first? A fluent summary can feel like understanding and hide the gaps you would have noticed yourself. Your own notes are what you measure Copilot against.

Check it

Accessibility Formatting Clean Data as a table gives it one clear header row that screen readers can announce. Give the table a meaningful name (Table Design → Table Name), and never show a problem value by colour alone: add a note in the Cleaning Log instead.

Critical thinking point: which number is right?

This table gives 958 confirmed cases in England for 2025. UKHSA's 2026 epidemiology page gives 959 for 2025, and says its figures are provisional and subject to change (UKHSA). The same historic page also reports 6,294 notifications of measles for 2025. Notified cases are offered testing to confirm the diagnosis, so notifications are not the same as confirmed cases, and the page says notifications for 2021 to 2025 are a provisional estimate because of a data collection issue.

  1. Which 2025 figure would you report, and how would you note the difference in your Cleaning Log's Data quality concerns?
  2. Ask Copilot: “What is the difference between measles notifications and confirmed cases?” Then check its answer against the UKHSA page. Which measure fits your research question?
  3. London has many more cases than the North East. Is that only because more people live there? What would you need from ONS to compare regions fairly?

Summary

Disclose

Name every tool and say what it did, e.g. “PubMed search string drafted with Microsoft Copilot, then checked and edited by [name].”

Protect

Use your UofG login. If you choose another platform, use it knowingly. Never upload personal, unpublished or restricted material.

Credit

Cite each dataset with its publisher, edition, URL, access date and licence, as in the Source Info sheet.

Verify

Every reference is found in a database; every claim matches its source; every cleaning step is logged and checked.
