Before you start

In Session 2 you turned a scientific message into images, quizzes and posts, with accessibility built in. Today we look at a different question: when does GenAI actually help with data analysis, and when is it faster and safer to do the work yourself?

How today works You will do every analysis step in Excel. Copilot is only a chat partner that explains, suggests and checks: it is your assistant, not your data analyst.

Set up

  1. Download bee_colonies.xlsx from Moodle and open it in Excel. In case you cannot use .xlsx due to compatibility issues, you can also work with the two .csv files.
  2. In your browser, go to copilot.microsoft.com and sign in with your UofG account.
  3. If a Copilot button or side pane appears inside Excel, keep it closed today. It is designed to work with the data in the open workbook (Microsoft), and today the analysis is yours.
  4. Open a blank Word document for your lab log.

Accessibility

Accessibility in data work means two things: Copilot's explanations must be understandable, and the tables and charts you make must work for everyone. Put it in the prompt and in your own Excel habits from the start.

Task 1 · Faster by hand?

You can run data analysis by yourselves. Test whether Copilot saves you any time, and where it could help instead. You are expected to time yourself: simply note the time when you start and finish the calculations, then do the same while using Copilot.

  1. In the colony sheet, filter state to [California].
  2. Calculate the mean, median and standard deviation of colony_lost_pct.
  3. Copy the same column of values into the prompt below and run it. Check Copilot's answer.
  4. Compare the efficiency between your computations and Copilot's assisted computations.
Example prompt (P-T-C-F-A + Accessibility)
PERSONA: You are a statistician helping a science student.
TASK: Calculate the mean, median and sample standard deviation of the values below.
CONTEXT: Percent of honey bee colonies lost per quarter in [California], 2015–2021. [One quarter has no value.]
FORMAT: A three-row table with values to 2 decimal places, then one sentence on how you calculated the standard deviation.
ACCURACY: Use all the listed values and no others. Tell me how many values you used. Show your working.
ACCESSIBILITY: Plain English; name each statistic in words, not only as a symbol.

Values:
[paste the colony_lost_pct values for your state]

Think it through

  1. What did you use to calculate these in Excel (or OpenOffice)? Did you use =AVERAGE, =MEDIAN and =STDEV.S? If you chose a different function for the standard deviation, does it give the sample or the population value, and which one fits these data?
  2. Do the two sets of answers match? If not, which is right, and why? (Look at how many values each used, and how the blank was treated.)
  3. Which was faster, once you count the time spent checking Copilot?
  4. Sort these jobs into “Excel does it”, “I do it” and “Copilot could help”, and note why:
    • (a) the mean of a column
    • (b) a formula with three conditions whose syntax you can't remember
    • (c) choosing a chart type
    • (d) understanding an error such as #N/A
    • (e) deciding whether a pattern supports a conclusion
Why start here? If a task is quick for you and needs checking anyway, Copilot adds a step rather than removing one. Keep your answers to question 4: the rest of the session tests them.

Task 2 · Describe the dataset: you vs Copilot

Write your own description of the dataset, then ask Copilot for one and compare. The combined, checked version is your data description. You will reuse it in Tasks 3 and 5.

  1. Look through all three sheets. In your lab log, write what one row is, what each column means (type and units), the time span, where values are missing, and anything that could trip up an analysis.
  2. Open a fresh chat, upload bee_colonies.xlsx (paperclip / “+” icon) and run the prompt below.
  3. Compare the two using the questions underneath. Merge them into one checked data description.
Example prompt (P-T-C-F-A + Accessibility)
PERSONA: You are a data analyst who documents datasets for other researchers.
TASK: Write a data description of the attached workbook.
CONTEXT: Public USDA data on honey bee colony loss and varroa mites. I will analyse it myself in Excel.
FORMAT:
1. What one row represents in each sheet.
2. A table of columns: name, data type, units, plain-English meaning.
3. Time span and places covered.
4. Missing values: where, and how many.
5. Anything that could trip up an analysis.
ACCURACY: Use only what is in the file, including the "about" sheet. If you are unsure of a definition or unit, say so. Do not guess.
ACCESSIBILITY: Plain English; define technical terms on first use; give the table as real text, not an image.

Think it through

  1. Did Copilot notice that the United States row is a national total, not a seventh state?
  2. Did it notice that percent lost is calculated differently for the United States (check the about sheet)?
  3. Did it find the gap in April–June 2019?
  4. Did it invent any unit, definition or number that is not in the file?
  5. What did you spot that Copilot missed, and what did Copilot spot that you missed?

Uploading data: today vs your future projects

Uploading this file is fine because it contains no personal data. Under the UK GDPR, personal data is information relating to a living person who can be identified, directly or indirectly. Truly anonymous data is not covered, but pseudonymised data still is (ICO).

Your future data may be interviews, survey answers, patient records or unpublished results. For those, use today's habit: describe the data to Copilot, don't upload it. Enterprise data protection keeps your prompts out of model training, but it doesn't make an upload allowed if your consent forms or ethics approval say otherwise.

Task 3 · Formula assistant

You can use Copilot for formulas that are fiddly to work out yourself, then test every one. You need three new columns for Task 4.

  1. Join varroa_pct onto the colony sheet (column I), matching on year, months and state.
  2. Convert the quarter text (e.g. “January-March”) into a date in column J, so Excel can plot the quarters in time order.
  3. Summarise: the average percent lost per quarter across the six states, leaving out the United States row.
How to use this prompt Run it once per job. Replace [describe what the formula should do] with one of the three jobs above, and replace [paste your data description from Task 2] with the data description in your lab log. That way Copilot uses your real sheet and column names.
Example prompt (P-T-C-F-A + Accessibility)
PERSONA: You are an Excel expert who helps scientists with data representation, interpretation and formula creation.
TASK: Write one Excel formula that [describe what the formula should do, e.g. returns varroa_pct from the varroa sheet for the same year, months and state as this row].
CONTEXT: I use [Excel for Microsoft 365 on Windows]. The formula goes in [cell I2 of the colony sheet] and will be filled down. My sheets and columns are described below.
FORMAT: The formula in a code block, then a plain-English explanation of each part, then one way it could fail.
ACCURACY: Use only the sheet and column names in my data description. Say which references need $ signs and why. Say what happens when the value it looks up is blank.
ACCESSIBILITY: Explain every function name in words; no jargon without a definition.

My data description:
[paste your data description from Task 2]

Then: explain it back

Chain prompt 2 (in a fresh chat)
TASK: Explain what this Excel formula does, step by step.
FORMAT: Numbered steps, then a worked example for one row.
ACCURACY: If the formula has an error, or gives a wrong result for blank cells, say so.
ACCESSIBILITY: Plain English.

Formula:
[paste the formula Copilot gave you]
Why a fresh chat? In the same chat, Copilot already “knows” what the formula was meant to do. A fresh chat has only the formula, so its explanation is a fairer test of what the formula actually does.

Test it

Critical thinking point: an average of percentages

Your average gives every state equal weight. But California typically has almost nine times as many colonies as Georgia (compare their colony_n values).

  1. Ask Copilot: “Is the simple average of these state percentages the same as the overall percent of colonies lost across the six states?”
  2. Check it yourself for one quarter: compare your average with SUM of colony_lost ÷ SUM of colony_max for the six states.
  3. Which number would you report to the public, and how would you describe it?

Task 4 · Build a two-axis chart

Chart percent of colonies lost and percent affected by varroa mites over time, for one state. You build the chart; Copilot helps only with the labels and the alt text.

  1. Filter the colony sheet to [your state]. Use your date (J), colony_lost_pct (G) and varroa_pct (I) from Task 3.
  2. Ask Copilot for a quarter label column with the prompt below, and add it in column K.
Example prompt (P-T-C-F-A + Accessibility)
PERSONA: You are an Excel expert who helps scientists make clear charts.
TASK: Write a formula that turns the date in column J into a label such as "2015 Q1".
CONTEXT: The labels will go on the horizontal axis of a line chart showing [percent of colonies lost and percent affected by varroa mites in California], quarter by quarter, 2015–2021.
FORMAT: The formula in a code block and a one-line explanation.
ACCURACY: Q1 is January–March, Q2 April–June, Q3 July–September, Q4 October–December. Tell me how to check the formula on three rows.
ACCESSIBILITY: Labels must be short and readable when the chart is narrow.

Build the chart

  1. Select the data. With your state still filtered, select the column headers and values of colony_lost_pct (G) and varroa_pct (I). Hold Ctrl (Mac: Cmd) to select the second column.
  2. Insert a line chart. Go to Insert → Line and choose the first line chart.
  3. Move varroa to the secondary axis. Click the chart, then Chart Design → Change Chart Type → Combo. Set both series to Line, tick Secondary Axis for varroa_pct, and select OK (Microsoft). If you can't see Combo (e.g. on some Mac versions), right-click the varroa line, choose Format Data Series and select Secondary Axis.
  4. Add your quarter labels. Go to Chart Design → Select Data. Under Horizontal (Category) Axis Labels, select Edit and select your labels in column K.
  5. Make it accessible. Chart title and axis titles with units, direct labels, a blue/orange palette, no 3D or shadows.

Alt text: save your chart as a picture, upload it to a fresh Copilot chat for alt text, and check the result with the alt-text questions from Session 2, Task 2.

Two axes, two stories

Each axis of a dual-axis chart can be scaled however you like, so the same data can be made to look related, or not. General audiences can misread these charts (ONS Digital blog; Datawrapper, 2018).

That is not the whole story. In July 2026, Datawrapper updated its 2018 article: it now allows dual-axis charts, because in some cases they are the best way to show the data, and readers used to them (e.g. in finance) are not misled. It still suggests considering alternatives for a general audience (Datawrapper, edit July 2026). Its new guide explains when dual axes work, for example when the two series have different units, and how to make them well, for example scaling both axes the same way and starting both at zero (Datawrapper guide, 2026).

  1. Change the secondary axis maximum (e.g. from 100 to 30). How does the story change? Which scale is fair to both measures?
  2. Who is your audience? Are they likely to be used to reading dual-axis charts?
  3. Ask Copilot: “When is a dual-axis chart a good choice to show the relationship between these two measures, and when is it not?” Compare its answer with the sources above.
  4. Compare your chart with one alternative, such as two charts side by side or a scatter plot of one measure against the other. Which shows your message more clearly for your audience, and why?

Task 5 · Interpret: you first, then Copilot

Write your own conclusion, then ask Copilot for its interpretation and a critique of yours. Decide whether its input added value.

  1. Write 3–4 sentences in your lab log on what your chart and averages show. You can add =CORREL between colony_lost_pct and varroa_pct for your state.
  2. Run the prompt below with summary numbers only, not the whole dataset.
  3. Compare Copilot's answer with yours using the questions underneath.
Why you first? If you read Copilot's interpretation first, it is hard to tell afterwards which ideas were yours. Writing first also gives you something to measure Copilot against.

Think it through

  1. Where did Copilot agree with you, and where not? Check each disagreement against your spreadsheet.
  2. Did it suggest explanations you hadn't thought of (e.g. season, other stressors, colonies added to replace losses)? Can your data support or rule them out?
  3. Did it add numbers, studies or facts that are not in your data? Can you verify them?
  4. Was this step worth it? Compare with your answers to Task 1, question 4.

Summary

Disclose

Say how you used Copilot, e.g. “Excel formulas drafted with Microsoft Copilot and tested by [name].”

Protect

Use your UofG login only. Describe personal or unpublished data to Copilot; never upload it.

Credit

Cite your data source and licence, e.g. USDA NASS via TidyTuesday (CC0).

Verify

Every number you report comes from your spreadsheet. “Copilot said so” is not a source.
